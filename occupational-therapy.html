✋ Occupational therapy

Mastering the everyday: hands, senses, and routines.

2–14 typical age range
45–60 min session length
In-network with 9 plans

Occupational therapy has the most misleading name in healthcare. For children, "occupation" means the job of being a kid: playing, drawing, dressing, eating lunch in a loud cafeteria, sitting through circle time. Our OTs treat the sensory and motor skills underneath those jobs.

Both of our occupational therapists are OTR/Ls with pediatric specialty practice, and our clinic rooms are built for this work: a sensory gym with a rock wall and swings, a fine-motor studio, and a quiet regulation room for kids who need the volume turned down before they can work.

What a plan covers

Inside occupational therapy at Tidepool

Sensory processing & regulation

Kids who seek intense input or collapse under ordinary input. We build a sensory diet the family can actually run, then fade our support as regulation becomes the child's own skill.

Fine motor & hand skills

Grasps, scissors, buttons, zippers, and the two-finger strength that handwriting borrows. Progress is measured in skills mastered, not minutes spent.

Handwriting

Print and cursive remediation built on posture, pencil grip, and letter formation, with home programs that take five minutes, not an hour.

Self-care routines

Dressing, tooth-brushing, toileting schedules, and morning routines that currently run the whole household. We re-engineer the routine, not the child.

Visual-motor & coordination

Ball skills, puzzles, copying from the board: the eye-hand teamwork that classroom work quietly assumes.

Parent coaching

Every OT plan includes structured parent sessions, because regulation strategies delivered only in the clinic are strategies your child never uses anywhere else.

Is this the right door?

Who we typically see

  • Meltdowns in predictable settings: stores, haircuts, car rides
  • Tags, seams, or certain clothing textures are unbearable
  • Your child avoids coloring, cutting, or utensils
  • Handwriting is illegible and practice at home ends in tears
  • Morning routine takes more than one adult to survive
  • Your child crashes, bumps, and seeks rough play constantly

Not sure your child fits these? The free consult exists exactly for that question. Twenty minutes, honest answer.

Our evidence baseline

  • Ayres-informed sensory integration, applied within activity-based sessions
  • The zones-of-regulation framework for self-awareness and vocabulary
  • Handwriting Without Tears as our foundational handwriting curriculum
  • Routine-based intervention for self-care: practice where the skill is needed

We track each child's progress with the same measures every visit and show you the graph at every plan review. If the line is flat for twelve weeks, we change the plan, not the explanation.

Common questions

Occupational therapy FAQ

Do we need a referral? +

No. California gives families direct access to therapy evaluations. If your insurance plan requires a physician referral for reimbursement, we will tell you during the benefits check and help you get one quickly.

How often will we come in? +

Most families start with one 45–60 minute session per week. Kids who need faster progress often do two. We re-check frequency at every twelve-week plan review rather than letting it drift.

What do sessions actually look like? +

Play-based and purposeful. Your child will experience it as games, obstacles, and stories. You will experience it as a clinician who can name exactly which skill they were targeting and how you can practice it at home in five minutes a day.

Can you work with my child's school? +

Yes. With your written OK we share goals and progress notes with teachers, join IEP meetings by request, and coach school staff on the same strategies we use in the clinic so the approach is consistent everywhere.

What if my child refuses to participate? +

That is information, not failure. Early sessions may look like relationship-building: showing your child the room, the toys, and the person. We would rather spend two sessions earning trust than one session forcing compliance.
